Somewhere in your organization, a team receives an approved request, finds a supplier, issues a purchase order, and closes the ticket. The price was reasonable, the order arrived on schedule, and every metric the team reports says the work went well. Meanwhile the organization committed capital without anyone asking whether the request should have been filled this way, from this supplier, on these terms. Nobody was supposed to ask. That question was never part of the job.

I call that function purchasing, and plenty of organizations that put procurement on the org chart are still running it. The distinction decides what a board can rely on. Purchasing is accountable for the transaction. Procurement is accountable for the process that produces transactions, strategic sourcing for the spend those transactions add up to, and category management for the outcome of an entire category. A board that believes it has the later functions while funding the first one is carrying a risk it has never priced.

## What Purchasing Is Accountable For

Purchasing turns an approved request into a delivered good or service. Its natural measures are cycle time and price against a quote, and those measures reward doing the request exactly as written. A team measured that way has no reason to challenge a request and no authority to do so if it wanted to. The function works as designed, which is the problem.

Military logistics makes the point cleanly. A supply clerk who fills requisitions accurately and on time is doing the job. That clerk does not decide what the battalion should stock, which suppliers sit in the line of supply, or how much exposure a single point of failure creates. Those decisions belong to someone with authority over the system, and in an organization with only purchasing, that someone does not exist.

## Where the Unmanaged Money Sits

Picture three departments buying the same managed service from the same vendor. Each negotiated its own agreement, each pays a different rate, and each renews on a different date. No one is hiding anything. The agreements were reasonable when each department signed them, and no one with a view across the departments was ever asked to look.

That pattern repeats in every direction. Contracts renew automatically because nobody owns the renewal calendar. Spend flows around the approved supplier because buying from it is slower than buying from someone else. A supplier that provides something the organization cannot operate without sits in the records as one vendor among hundreds, with no flag on it. Each of these is small when viewed alone, and together they make up a large share of what the organization spends and very little of what it manages.

## What the Board Cannot See

Finance reports spend by budget line, which tells the board who spent the money and not who received it. A board that asks how much the organization pays its ten largest suppliers, which contracts renew in the next twelve months, which suppliers it could not replace within ninety days, and who holds the renewal calendar usually gets an answer assembled by hand over several weeks. Sometimes it gets no answer. Those are risk questions, and they belong to the board as much as capital allocation or cyber exposure does.

The cost of that gap rarely shows up as a line item. It shows up when a supplier fails or raises prices at renewal, and the organization discovers the dependency at the same moment it discovers the exposure. A leadership team can explain a failure it could not have predicted. It has a much harder time explaining one it could have seen if anyone had been asked to look.

## How to Tell Which Function You Have

Four questions to the person running the function will settle it in one meeting. Ask what the organization spends with its largest supplier across every department, and see whether the answer comes from a report or a research project. Ask who owns contract renewal dates, and listen for a name instead of a shrug. Ask when the team learns that a department wants to buy something new, before the supplier is chosen or after. Ask which suppliers the organization could not replace within ninety days.

If the answers are a research project, nobody, after, and no idea, you have purchasing. That is useful to know, and it is not an indictment of the team. Those answers describe the scope someone gave them.

## Why the Function Stays Where It Is

Purchasing persists for reasons that make sense from the inside. It reports into finance or operations as an administrative service, so leadership evaluates it on throughput. Savings claimed from negotiations get counted as cost avoidance, which is hard to audit and easy to believe. And the people running it have no mandate to do more, so they do the work they were given and do it well.

That last point is the one executives need to take seriously. The function is not failing, and swapping its manager or renaming it will not change what it does. The scope of the function is a leadership decision, made by whoever decided what it would be accountable for. Changing the outcome means changing that decision.

## What the Sponsor Has to Give

The first move is small and bounded. Ask the purchasing team for a single extract: twelve months of payments, grouped by parent supplier, with each supplier tagged to a spend category. Give them ninety days and a named executive who will read the result. That is the entire ask, and it needs no new headcount and no reorganization.

The result will be uncomfortable in a useful way. You will see how many suppliers the organization pays, how concentrated the spend is, and how much of it sits outside any contract. You will also find out whether your payment data is clean enough to support anything further, which tells you how long the second stage will take. Once that picture exists, the next question is who owns the process that produced it, and that is where the second post in this series begins.
